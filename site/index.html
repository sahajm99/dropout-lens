Who is in the data, and how does it end?

Of every 100 students who enrolled, … had left by the end of their course.

Each tile is one student in every hundred of the … who enrolled at a Portuguese polytechnic between 2008 and 2019, in the dataset Realinho, Machado, Baptista and Martins published as Predicting Student Dropout and Academic Success (in Data) and on the UCI Machine Learning Repository under a Creative Commons Attribution licence. This copy carries … features per student, recorded at admission and through the first year, and one outcome taken at the normal end of the course: … dropped out, … were still enrolled and … graduated. Every number on this page is read from a file a pipeline wrote from that dataset; none is typed in.

The same file sat under two of my graduate course projects at the University of North Texas: a Fundamentals of AI project that plotted it and trained six classifiers, and an Empirical Analysis project a semester later that ran chi-square tests, t-tests and ANOVA over it. The first dropped every Enrolled student, used both semesters' results as features and reported a random forest's accuracy on a random split; the second coded Enrolled together with Graduate, and its written conclusions were template text that did not follow from its own p-values. Neither figure is comparable with anything below. This page asks their questions again and measures every answer under one fixed split, with intervals, effect sizes and the limits stated.

Enrolled is the outcome to read carefully. It does not mean the student stayed; it means that at the normal end of the course they had neither graduated nor left, so the course was still in progress. It is kept as its own class throughout, because calling it a success or a failure would be a guess, and when a question is simply dropout or not, an enrolled student counts as not having dropped out. Later intake years carry more of it, since their students had less time to finish.

What do the tests say?

The course project ran a chi-square test per variable and read off the p-values. With … rows nearly everything clears the conventional line: of the … tests here, … stay under it after the Holm adjustment, which holds the chance of a single false positive across the whole family rather than test by test. Significance therefore says almost nothing about which variables matter. Effect size does. Each categorical variable is tested against the three-way outcome and reported with bias-corrected Cramér's V; age and the semester grades are tested with ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis and reported with eta squared. Raw and adjusted p-values sit side by side in every table.

A p-value answers whether a difference this large would be surprising if there were none; with thousands of rows a trivial difference is surprising. Cramér's V and eta squared answer how large the association is on a fixed scale, and that is the number to compare across variables. For age as a number, eta squared is …: the three outcome groups differ in age at enrolment, and age accounts for that share of its variance. The first-semester grade's … is larger and leaks, like the approval band it summarises.

What can a model predict, and when?

One row in five is held out, stratified on the three-way outcome, and never touched until the end: … rows to fit and choose on, … to report on. Four models are fitted for each of two targets, dropout or not and the three-way outcome, and the choice between settings is made by five-fold cross-validation on the training rows alone, so the test rows are seen once per model. Every test metric carries a bootstrap interval from a thousand resamples of the test rows.

Each model is fitted twice. The at-enrolment set uses what was known on enrolment day plus the two administrative flags. The after-first-semester set adds the first-semester unit counts and grade, and it leaks: those columns are recorded at the end of the first semester and are partly the outcome itself, since a student who passed nothing is most of the way out of the door. The two are drawn side by side because the gap between them is the finding.

What this cannot tell you

Each of these applies to every number above it.

  • One institution. Every number describes one Portuguese polytechnic's records over one span of years. Nothing here transfers to another institution or country without being measured there.
  • The outcome is measured at the normal course end. Enrolled means the student had not finished when the record was taken, not that they stayed. It is ambiguous between a slow pass and an eventual exit, and later intake cohorts carry more of it because their students had less time to finish. The file has no year column, so which cohorts are the recent ones cannot be confirmed.
  • First-semester features leak. Every figure with the accent rule uses columns recorded at the end of the first semester, which are partly the outcome. The number it produces is a reading of the first semester, not a prediction from enrolment, and it is never the headline.
  • Tuition and debtor status are administrative states. Fees up to date and debtor are kept in the at-enrolment set because the dataset lists them there, but both are recorded during the course and typically flip when a student stops attending. The at-enrolment metrics are therefore more flattering than a true at-admission model would be, and the estimator holds both at their reference level rather than asking for them.
  • One course has no first-year record. Most of the students in Animation and Multimedia Design have every curricular column at zero, across seven of the ten intake years. That is a gap in the export, not a course in which students enrolled in nothing. Those rows are kept as their own approval band, no zero is imputed, and the after-first-semester models learn the course partly through the missing record.
  • No causal claims. Every association here is observational. A scholarship does not cause graduation because scholarship holders graduate more often; the grant is awarded on need and kept on passing, which selects the students who hold it.
  • Intervals are not significance. An interval says how precisely a share is estimated from this many rows. Two intervals that overlap do not show there is no difference, and two that do not overlap are not a test; the tests are in their own section, with effect sizes beside them.
  • Subgroups under thirty are suppressed. A cell with fewer than thirty students carries no estimate at all, and a cell under a hundred is drawn greyed. Every figure carries its own table, so the count behind each claim is one click away.
  • Parents' background and nationality are unused. Parents' qualification and occupation, and nationality beyond the Portuguese-or-international flag, appear in no chart or model: their coded levels have no verified label mapping here, and most are too thin to estimate.
  • Two admission-grade columns are absent. This copy carries … features; the UCI release lists two more, the previous qualification grade and the admission grade, which are missing from the copy the course projects used. A model with them would likely do better at enrolment than anything here.
